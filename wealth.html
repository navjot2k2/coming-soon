Wealth Management

Understanding wealth management in Canada.

A plain-language primer on the tools Canadians use to grow, protect, and transfer wealth — from registered accounts to insurance-based investment products.

Building blocks

Core tools Canadians use.

Wealth management isn't one product — it's a mix of registered accounts, insurance, and investment structures chosen to fit your income, tax situation, and risk tolerance.

Investment

Segregated fund contracts

Insurance-based investment funds, similar to mutual funds, but issued as individual insurance contracts with maturity and death benefit guarantees (typically 75–100% of deposits).

  • Potential creditor protection when a named beneficiary (spouse, child, parent, or grandchild) is designated
  • Bypasses probate, so proceeds can pass directly to beneficiaries faster and more privately
  • Reset options can lock in market gains on the guaranteed value
  • Generally carry higher fees (MERs) than comparable mutual funds for the added guarantees
Registered accounts

TFSA vs. RRSP

The two foundational registered accounts for most Canadians, each suited to a different stage of saving.

  • TFSA: contributions aren't tax-deductible, but growth and withdrawals are completely tax-free
  • RRSP: contributions are tax-deductible now, but withdrawals are taxed as income later — best when your working-years tax rate exceeds your retirement rate
  • Unused TFSA and RRSP room both carry forward indefinitely
  • Annual limits are set federally and indexed most years — worth checking current-year figures before contributing
Insurance

Critical illness & accidental insurance

Living-benefit coverage that pays out on a defined health event, distinct from life insurance which pays on death.

  • Critical illness insurance: a lump-sum payment on diagnosis of a covered condition, usable for anything — treatment, income replacement, debt
  • Accidental death & dismemberment (AD&D): a lump-sum benefit for accidental death or serious injury, often sold as a low-cost add-on to a base policy
  • Disability insurance covers loss of income more broadly than AD&D, which only responds to accidents
  • Useful as a standalone gap-filler alongside employer group benefits, which are often capped or non-portable
Insurance

Permanent life insurance as an asset

Whole and universal life policies build cash value over time in addition to providing a death benefit.

  • Cash value grows on a tax-advantaged basis and can be accessed through policy loans or withdrawals
  • Often used for estate liquidity — covering final taxes so other assets (a business, a property) don't need to be sold
  • Universal life offers more flexibility in premiums and underlying investments than whole life
  • Best suited to long time horizons; surrendering early can mean giving up much of the value
Business owners

Corporate-owned life insurance

A strategy used by incorporated business owners to hold a life insurance policy inside the corporation.

  • Death benefit proceeds can flow to shareholders through the corporation's capital dividend account, largely tax-free
  • Cash value can serve as a source of corporate collateral for a policy loan or leveraging strategy
  • Often paired with a shareholder or buy-sell agreement to fund a smooth ownership transition
  • Requires coordination between a licensed insurance advisor and the corporation's accountant
Estate

Estate & beneficiary planning

How assets are titled and who is named as beneficiary often matters as much as how much is saved.

  • Registered accounts and insurance products with named beneficiaries generally bypass probate
  • Naming a beneficiary "in trust for" a minor requires care — funds are usually locked until the age of majority
  • A will should be reviewed after major life events: marriage, divorce, a new child, or a move between provinces
  • Powers of attorney (property and personal care) are a separate but essential piece of the plan

At a glance

Segregated funds vs. mutual funds.

Feature Segregated fund Mutual fund
Maturity guarantee Typically 75–100% of deposits None
Death benefit guarantee Typically 75–100% of deposits None (market value only)
Creditor protection Possible with an eligible named beneficiary Generally not available
Bypasses probate Yes, with a named beneficiary No
Fees (MER) Generally higher Generally lower
Issued by Insurance company Investment fund company
